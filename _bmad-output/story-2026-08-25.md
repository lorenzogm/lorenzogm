**Story Type:** pixar_story_spine

**Target Audience:** Software engineers, tech leads and engineering managers already experimenting with AI agents, who have hit the "where does a human still approve?" wall.

## Story Structure

### Opening Hook

> Once upon a time, the development life cycle had two modes: planning and building. Everything interesting happened in the gap between them, and the gap was where context went to die.

The hook reuses the v1 premise as a fairy-tale opening so returning readers feel continuity and new readers get the problem in one sentence.

### Core Narrative

v1 solved the *context* problem by making the backlog a first-class middle layer. It did not solve two others: design was outside the loop, and human involvement was everywhere and nowhere at once. v2 keeps the spine (Planning → Backlog → Build) and adds Experience Design as a parallel branch, names every step as a command, and reduces "human in the loop" to five defensible approval gates — which is what makes eight unattended steps safe to hand to the Software Factory.

### Key Story Beats

Pixar Story Spine mapping:

1. **Once upon a time** — the life cycle had two modes, and the gap between them ate the context.
2. **Every day** — teams patched the gap with docs, meetings and tribal memory.
3. **Until one day** — v1 shipped: three layers, backlog promoted to first-class. It worked.
4. **Because of that** — agents got more capable, and two cracks appeared: design was outside the loop, and nobody could say where a human still had to approve.
5. **Because of that** — "human in the loop" degraded from a process into a slogan.
6. **Until finally** — v2: Experience Design joins as a parallel branch, every step becomes a named command, and five explicit approval gates replace ambient supervision.
7. **And ever since** — eight of twelve steps carry 🤖 and can be handed to the Software Factory. The human's job shifts from *doing the steps* to *clearing the gates*.

### Emotional Arc

- **Opening:** recognition, mild fatigue — "yes, the gap, I live there."
- **Turning point:** discomfort — the reader realises they cannot actually name where their own team approves things.
- **Peak:** relief and clarity — five gates, defended one by one, plus an explicit defence of where gates are *absent*.
- **Close:** productive unease — "get this wrong in either direction and here is what happens."

### Resolution/Call to Action

The closing reframes the field's question: not "can the agent do this step?" but "which steps still deserve a human signature, and which ones only feel like they do?" The implicit CTA is to audit your own flow against the five-gate table, and to wait for the Software Factory article for the automation half.

## Story Elements Analysis

### Character/Voice

First person, opinionated practitioner. Short declarative sentences, one idea per paragraph, occasional dry punchline ("an unsupervised intern with commit access"). Consistent with the existing lorenzogm.com voice: no hype, no time estimates, no "revolutionary".

The protagonist is not a person — it is the process itself. The reader is cast as the one holding the pen at the five gates.

### Conflict/Tension

The central tension is **autonomy vs. accountability**. Every capability the agent gains removes a natural checkpoint that used to exist by accident (you had to be there, so you saw it). v2 argues that checkpoints must now be *designed* rather than inherited.

Secondary tension: the temptation to add more gates. Addressed head-on — "most requests for more approvals are requests for a better spec wearing a disguise."

### Transformation/Change

- Process: two modes → three layers → three layers with a design branch and explicit gates.
- Reader's role: operator of every step → approver at five decision points.
- Human involvement: ambient and unmeasured → named, counted, and defended.

### Emotional Touchpoints

- "Keep a human in the loop is not a process. It is a slogan." — the challenge that makes the reader sit up.
- "The Factory drafts the tickets overnight, you approve them with coffee." — concrete, low-drama picture of the payoff.
- "An idea cannot be developed unattended, because the missing information lives in your head." — validates the reader's value.
- "This is the last moment where changing your mind is cheap." — the stakes line for gate 3.
- "You wake up to a merged PR that solves a problem nobody had." — the fear line at the close.

### Key Messages

1. v2 is an evolution of v1, not a replacement — the three layers still hold.
2. Experience Design is a first-class parallel branch that converges on the same backlog through a prototype.
3. Five approval gates, placed where mistakes propagate or where work leaves your workspace.
4. The absence of gates is a design decision too, and worth defending.
5. Eight of twelve steps can run unattended in the Software Factory — dedicated article coming.

>
> It broke in two places. Design was outside the loop, so the flow assumed someone had already decided what the experience should be. And the more the agents could actually do, the less obvious it became where a human still had to say yes.
>
> v2 keeps the spine and adds three things: Experience Design as a parallel branch that converges on the same backlog through a prototype, a named command for every step, and five explicit approval gates — `/plan-tickets`, `/plan-prototype-to-tickets`, `/dev-implementation-plan`, `/dev-code-review`, `/dev-ship`.
>
> Three of those gates catch scope and approach mistakes before they multiply. Two of them guard the moment work leaves the agent's hands and becomes the team's problem. Everything else runs unattended — eight of twelve steps — which is what makes a Software Factory possible in the first place.

## Usage Guidelines

### Best Channels

- Primary: lorenzogm.com blog (EN + ES), diagram as the social card.
- LinkedIn: the short version plus the gates table as an image — the table is the most screenshot-friendly asset.
- Internal enablement: the diagram alone works as a one-slide onboarding artefact for a team adopting the flow.

### Audience Considerations

- Readers of v1 need the delta up front — the "What Changed From v1" section should survive any cut.
- Readers new to the series need the diagram before the step-by-step walkthrough, which is why the image sits high in the article.
- Sceptical engineering managers care most about the gates table; sceptical engineers care most about the *absence* of gates. Both objections are answered explicitly.

### Tone AND Voice Notes

- No time estimates, no adoption-effort promises.
- Command names always in backticks, symbols (🤖 / ✋) always in the same order.
- Spanish version is a translation of voice, not of words — commands, `epic`, `spec`, `backlog`, `quality gates` and `PR` stay in English because that is how the audience speaks.

### Adaptation Suggestions

- A five-slide deck: one slide per gate, with the "what the human is deciding" column as the slide title.
- A checklist version: "audit your flow — can you name your gates?"
- The diagram can be re-cut per group (Planning / XD / Development) for a carousel post.
